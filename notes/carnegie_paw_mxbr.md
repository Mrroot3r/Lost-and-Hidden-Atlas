## What this batch is

Twenty-three Carnegie libraries of western Pennsylvania (one, Johnstown, in the central region), part of the pass the owner asked for to put the buildings Andrew Carnegie and the Rockefellers paid for on the map. They include the steel-town libraries Carnegie built and endowed himself (Braddock, the first to open in the United States, in 1889; Homestead; Carnegie borough; and the site of Duquesne's, torn down in 1968), seven of Pittsburgh's own neighbourhood branches (Lawrenceville, Homewood, Mount Washington, South Side and West End still working; Hazelwood standing empty; Wylie Avenue now the First Muslim Mosque of Pittsburgh) and the site of the East Liberty branch, and town libraries from Bradford, Butler, Connellsville, Corry, Edgewood, McKeesport, Midland, Oakmont, Oil City and Swissvale. Johnstown's is the Cambria Library, rebuilt by Carnegie after the 1889 flood and now the Johnstown Flood Museum. Beaver Falls, Allegheny (the North Side), the Carnegie Institute in Oakland and Pottsville were already on the Atlas and were left alone.

Sources: Wikipedia's list of Carnegie libraries in Pennsylvania and the libraries' own Wikipedia articles, the Carnegie Library of Pittsburgh's branch history pages, SAH Archipedia, the libraries' and museums' own sites (hours), Pittsburgh History and Landmarks Foundation plaques read through HMdb, and local papers. Pins are geocoder or OpenStreetMap points, each looked at on the aerial; Duquesne's site is the OpenStreetMap point on Library Place.

Not checked: hours for the five working Pittsburgh branches, because the Carnegie Library of Pittsburgh's pages load their hours by script and could not be read here (each place says so and points to the library's site); hours for Butler (its site would not load) and Corry (the restaurant's site gives none). Grants where sources disagree are given both ways (Carnegie borough, Bradford, Connellsville, Oil City, Homewood). Johnstown's last year as a library is 1971 (Wikipedia) or 1973 (the Johnstown Area Heritage Association). Duquesne's facts beyond Wikipedia's list come from a local history blog. Parking was not found for several and is left out where no source gave it.

Red brick: "yes" for Duquesne, South Side, Carnegie borough and Corry from sources, and for Homestead, Hazelwood, Homewood, Lawrenceville, West End, Swissvale, Bradford and Oakmont from the Commons pictures looked at. Oakmont's walls disagree: a Shady Ave article says limestone, while the 2015 picture shows red brick gables with stone trim; the place gives both. Mount Washington's brick is painted white, so it is "no". Stone buildings are "no"; the rest are "?".

Pictures: twenty places have a Commons picture chosen by looking at the finder's sheet. Duquesne (torn down), Wylie Avenue (nothing on Commons) and Butler (the named pictures are of Butler, Indiana) have none.
